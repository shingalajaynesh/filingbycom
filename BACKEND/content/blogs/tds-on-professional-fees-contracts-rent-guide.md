# TDS on Professional Fees, Contracts and Rent: A Practical Working Guide for Business Owners

If you run a growing business, vendor payments eventually stop being a simple accounts task. One invoice is for a chartered accountant. Another is for office interiors. A third is for monthly office rent. Somebody in the team asks, "Should we deduct TDS at 1%, 2%, 10%, or not at all?" That is where most businesses start mixing up Section 194C, 194I and 194J.

The confusion is understandable. Vendors often use casual labels such as "consultancy", "support", "retainer", "services", "maintenance" or "project fee". Those labels are useful for billing, but they do not decide the TDS section by themselves. The real test is the nature of payment, the contract behind it, the status of the payer, and the threshold that applies in that year.

This guide is written for founders, finance heads, accounts teams, consultants, agencies, contractors, MSMEs and startup operators who need a practical answer, not a textbook answer. It focuses on resident payments commonly reviewed under the Income-tax Act, 1961 sections 194C, 194I and 194J. Where relevant, it also flags the 2026 transition to the Income Tax Act, 2025 so the reader does not apply an old form name blindly.

> **Important:** The current Income Tax Department guidance says the governing law during the transition depends on the **earlier of credit or payment**. If that earlier event happened on or before **31 March 2026**, the older section references such as 194C, 194I and 194J continue to matter. If the earlier event happened on or after **1 April 2026**, the new Act structure applies on the portal. The commercial classification logic is still useful, but the portal naming and form mapping may differ.

## The quick answer: what usually falls under 194C, 194I and 194J

In practice, these three sections usually sit in different buckets:

| Section | Usually covers | Typical rate | Threshold currently reflected in departmental guidance | Common examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 194C | Carrying out work under a contract | 1% if payee is an individual or HUF, 2% in other cases | Rs. 30,000 for a single payment or Rs. 1,00,000 in aggregate in a financial year | Fabrication, repair contracts, printing jobs, manpower supply, interior work, transport contracts |
| 194I | Rent for use of assets | 2% for plant or machinery, 10% for land, building, furniture or fittings | Rs. 50,000 per month or part of a month under current departmental update | Office rent, warehouse rent, renting furniture, taking equipment on lease |
| 194J | Professional or technical services, royalty, director's fees, non-compete payments | 2% for technical services and certain film royalty situations, 10% for professional fees and most other covered sums | Rs. 50,000 in a financial year for covered categories other than director's fees | CA fees, legal fees, architect fees, software consulting, design retainers, specialist advisory work |

This table is the starting point, not the final answer. If the agreement and the invoice tell different stories, the agreement usually matters more.

## Why businesses misclassify these payments

Most TDS errors do not happen because the law is too difficult. They happen because the payment process is built around speed rather than classification.

A founder approves a payment on WhatsApp. Accounts books the vendor using the first section used last quarter. Nobody reads the agreement again. Then the same vendor starts doing a different type of work, or the annual value crosses a threshold, or the payee category changes. The result is a mismatch that only becomes visible when the quarterly return is prepared or when the deductee complains that credit is not appearing correctly.

There are four recurring reasons for mistakes:

### The invoice description is treated as final

"Consulting fees" may genuinely be professional fees under 194J. But some vendors use the word loosely even when they are actually carrying out a project contract. The accounts team should never rely only on the heading line.

### Mixed invoices are not split or reviewed

One invoice may contain design advice, execution work and reimbursable travel costs. If the business does not review the breakdown before payment, the TDS section becomes guesswork.

### The individual or HUF rule is forgotten

Individuals and HUFs are not automatically outside TDS. For 194C and 194J, the turnover test in the immediately preceding financial year matters. Separate provisions such as 194IB and 194M can also become relevant in some cases.

### Teams repeat what worked for another vendor

This is common in MSMEs. A contractor is paid under 194C, so the next service provider is also tagged under 194C without checking whether the work is actually professional advice. That shortcut creates avoidable correction work later.

## How to decide between 194C, 194I and 194J

The safest way to classify a payment is to ask one question first: **What exactly is the payer buying?**

If the payer is buying the use of property, premises, machinery, furniture or similar assets, start with 194I.

If the payer is buying execution of work under a contract, start with 194C.

If the payer is buying specialised professional or technical expertise, start with 194J.

That sounds simple, but the real distinction is easier to see with examples.

### When 194J usually applies

Section 194J is commonly reviewed for services that depend mainly on specialised knowledge or professional judgment.

Examples:

- Chartered accountant audit or tax advisory fees
- Advocate or law firm professional charges
- Architect or interior designer concept fees
- Engineering design consultancy
- Specialist software or technical consulting
- Director's sitting fees or professional fees

If the value of the payment lies in expertise rather than execution of physical or operational work, 194J is often the first section to test.

### When 194C usually applies

Section 194C is usually relevant where the vendor is carrying out work under a contract.

Examples:

- Office fit-out or renovation contract
- Printing and packaging work
- Annual maintenance contract where the emphasis is execution
- Labour supply contract
- Fabrication or installation contract
- Event execution contract

A useful working test is this: if the client cares mainly about completion of a defined job, not about receiving expert advice as such, 194C often becomes more relevant.

### When 194I usually applies

Section 194I applies when the business is paying rent for use of assets.

Examples:

- Monthly rent for office premises
- Warehouse or godown rent
- Rent for furniture used in a leased office
- Hire charges for plant or machinery where the payment is for use of the asset

The rate depends on the asset category. Current departmental guidance reflects 2% for plant and machinery and 10% for land, building, furniture and fittings.

## Individual and HUF applicability: the point many small businesses miss

For 194C and 194J, the rule is not simply "companies deduct, individuals do not". The Income Tax Department guidance says individuals and HUFs must also deduct if their turnover in the immediately preceding financial year crossed the tax-audit trigger relevant to business or profession.

In practical terms:

- A company, LLP, partnership firm or other regular business payer generally has to evaluate these sections in the ordinary course.
- An individual or HUF should check the immediately preceding year's turnover before assuming TDS is not required under 194C or 194J.
- For rent and certain high-value personal or business payments by non-audit individuals or HUFs, special sections such as 194IB or 194M may still need review.

This is why founders should not build policy around assumptions like "I am still a proprietorship, so TDS does not apply." Entity type helps, but it is not the whole test.

## Thresholds and rates in one practical comparison

The rate is important, but applying the wrong section and then arguing over 1% or 2% is the wrong order of thinking. Section selection comes first.

| Payment scenario | Section to test first | Threshold and rate to keep in mind | Practical note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CA firm monthly retainer of Rs. 75,000 | 194J | Threshold currently reflected at Rs. 50,000 per year; rate commonly 10% for professional fees | This is a classic professional-fee case unless facts show otherwise |
| Office renovation contract of Rs. 1,80,000 with a contractor company | 194C | Threshold Rs. 30,000 single payment or Rs. 1,00,000 aggregate; rate 2% if payee is not individual/HUF | The job is execution-oriented, so 194C is usually reviewed first |
| Monthly office rent of Rs. 80,000 for premises | 194I | Threshold currently reflected at Rs. 50,000 per month; rate 10% for land/building | Office rent is not a professional fee merely because the lessor is a company |
| Generator or plant hire charges of Rs. 60,000 per month | 194I | Threshold currently reflected at Rs. 50,000 per month; rate 2% for plant/machinery | Check whether it is true hire of equipment or part of a broader works contract |
| Specialist technical consultant billing Rs. 1,20,000 | 194J | Threshold currently reflected at Rs. 50,000 per year; rate 2% for technical services or 10% depending on category | Read the scope carefully before choosing between professional and technical treatment |

## Two concrete examples businesses face every month

### Example 1: finance consultant on a monthly retainer

A startup hires a finance consultant for monthly MIS review, pricing support and investor data-room preparation. The consultant raises one invoice every month for Rs. 75,000.

This is usually a 194J review case because the business is paying for specialised advisory input. If the consultant is a resident and the threshold has been crossed, the payer normally deducts TDS at the applicable 194J rate.

What should the accounts team preserve?

- Engagement letter or retainer agreement
- Invoice showing nature of service
- PAN and onboarding data of the consultant
- Internal note showing why the payment is treated under 194J

This one-page note becomes useful later if somebody new joins the accounts team.

### Example 2: office interior work with milestone billing

An MSME gives a contractor a work order for office interiors. The value is Rs. 4,50,000, to be billed in three milestones. The contractor is responsible for execution, labour coordination and delivery.

This is usually reviewed under 194C because the core obligation is carrying out work. If the vendor is a company or partnership firm, the 2% rate under 194C is commonly checked. If the payee were an individual or HUF, the 1% rate would typically be reviewed instead.

The mistake many teams make here is calling the vendor a "consultant" in the ledger just because the GST registration description contains the word "services". The work order tells the better story.

## Rent of land and building versus plant and equipment

Section 194I deserves more care than it usually gets because the rate changes with the asset.

If the payment is for:

- land or building, including office premises, warehouse, shop, factory building, furniture or fittings, the commonly cited rate is 10%;
- plant or machinery, the commonly cited rate is 2%.

This difference matters in real life. A business may rent a furnished office and also separately hire a diesel generator or industrial machine. Treating both lines at one uniform rate without checking the nature of asset can create a short-deduction problem.

### A simple rent example

Suppose a company pays:

- Rs. 90,000 per month for office premises
- Rs. 18,000 per month for separate furniture hire
- Rs. 55,000 per month for a plant or equipment lease

The land/building line and furniture/fittings line sit in the 10% bucket commonly associated with 194I. The plant or machinery line usually sits in the 2% bucket. The threshold should still be checked carefully with the current monthly test reflected in departmental updates.

Do not merge everything just because one landlord or one lessor raised the bill.

## Mixed invoices and reimbursements: handle with discipline, not shortcuts

This is one of the most disputed areas in day-to-day TDS work.

Imagine an architect raises an invoice with:

- professional fee: Rs. 1,20,000
- travel reimbursement: Rs. 18,500
- out-of-pocket printing: Rs. 4,200

Some businesses deduct TDS on the full invoice. Others deduct only on the professional fee line. The safer commercial approach is not to guess. Review:

- what the contract says about reimbursements;
- whether the reimbursement is separately identified;
- whether it is backed by third-party bills;
- whether the vendor incurred the cost as a pure pass-through or as part of its own service obligation.

If the paperwork is weak, gross-basis deduction is often the lower-risk operational choice. If the paperwork is strong and the split is genuine, the business may take a more specific position after review. What matters most is consistency and documentation. A casual verbal explanation at month-end is never enough.

> **Warning:** The most expensive TDS habit is excluding part of the invoice without a documented basis. If you want to take a narrower TDS base, make sure the contract, invoice format and support papers all point in the same direction.

## Time of deduction: do not wait for actual bank payment if the expense is already credited

For these sections, TDS is generally deducted at the **earlier of credit or payment**.

That means if:

- the expense is booked in March, but
- the actual bank payment happens in April,

the obligation usually arises on the booking date if the credit event happened first.

This catches many startups because they finalise books at month-end, post expenses, but assume TDS can wait until the vendor is paid. That assumption is risky.

A simple internal control helps:

1. Review every vendor ledger booked in the month.
2. Flag ledgers likely to attract 194C, 194I or 194J.
3. Post TDS at the same time as the expense or liability entry.
4. Reconcile before payment run.

If your books are passed by one person and payments are released by another, make TDS status a mandatory approval field.

## TAN requirement and when it matters

The Income Tax Department's TAN guidance is clear on the broad rule: a person responsible for deducting TDS generally needs a TAN. That TAN is then quoted in statements and related TDS correspondence.

For the business situations covered in this article, the usual assumption is:

- if you are deducting under the ordinary TAN-based sections such as 194C, 194I or 194J, you should check that the business has a valid TAN and it is active on the e-filing system;
- special carve-outs exist for sections such as 194IA, 194IB, 194M and certain 194S cases where PAN may be used instead of TAN, but those are not substitutes for the normal 194C/194I/194J workflow.

If your business has started deducting TDS but has not completed the TAN setup cleanly, fix that first. Otherwise everything downstream becomes harder: payment, return filing, certificate generation and correction statements.

## Deposit process: what the business should do after deduction

For payments governed by the older 1961 Act workflow, the deductor generally deposits TDS through the tax payment system using the relevant challan route tied to TAN.

The current departmental FAQ on tax payments says the broad due date remains:

- within 7 days from the end of the month of deduction in general;
- for March deductions, by 30 April for non-government deductors;
- specific challan-cum-statement situations such as 194IA, 194IB, 194M and 194S follow separate 30-day rules.

For a normal company, LLP or firm handling 194C, 194I and 194J deductions, the practical workflow usually looks like this:

### Step 1: close the vendor ledger for the month

Freeze the payment list and identify the entries where TDS was required.

### Step 2: reconcile PAN, section and rate

Do not create the challan until the section mapping is checked. Wrong section coding leads to avoidable correction work.

### Step 3: create the tax payment through the portal process

Use the TAN-based payment route and preserve the challan details.

### Step 4: update the accounting records immediately

The challan reference should sit in the same monthly TDS file as the vendor-wise calculation.

### Step 5: prepare the quarterly statement from the same control sheet

If the monthly sheet is wrong, the return will be wrong. Most TDS return stress comes from weak month-end discipline, not from the return utility itself.

## Return forms, quarter deadlines and Form 16A

This is the part where 2026 transition language matters.

For resident non-salary payments governed by the traditional 1961 Act workflow, businesses have long associated quarterly reporting with **Form 26Q**. The standard quarter deadlines commonly used are:

| Quarter | Period | Due date commonly followed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q1 | April to June | 31 July |
| Q2 | July to September | 31 October |
| Q3 | October to December | 31 January |
| Q4 | January to March | 31 May |

The Income Tax portal's forms catalogue now maps **Form 26Q / Form 140** in the newer system. So if your consultant or software says "Form 140", do not panic and assume the substance changed. First check which tax year and which governing Act applies to the relevant payment or credit.

Form 16A remains the practical certificate businesses talk about for non-salary TDS. Departmental guidance states that it is issued after processing of the statement and is generally to be issued within 15 days from the due date of the relevant TDS statement.

That means your TDS job is not over when the challan is paid. The process continues until:

- the statement is filed;
- defaults are checked;
- Form 16A is downloaded from TRACES or the relevant workflow;
- the deductee gets the certificate.

## Consequences of getting it wrong

TDS defaults hurt twice. The first hit is financial. The second is operational.

Based on current departmental guidance, businesses should keep these consequences in mind:

- interest under section 201 for failure to deduct or delay in deposit;
- 1% per month or part of a month for failure to deduct, until deduction;
- 1.5% per month or part of a month for delay in deposit after deduction;
- late fee under section 234E at Rs. 200 per day for late filing of the statement, capped at the TDS amount;
- possible penalty exposure under section 271H for failure to file or for incorrect statements;
- possible penalty for failure to issue TDS certificates;
- assessee-in-default consequences in appropriate cases.

This is why businesses should correct classification problems early. Once one quarter is filed wrongly, the issue spills into vendor disputes, correction statements, Form 26AS mismatches and year-end reconciliation headaches.

## Common classification mistakes

### Treating every non-salary vendor as 194J

This often happens in professional-service businesses. Execution contracts then get short-deducted or over-deducted.

### Booking office rent as a service expense

The ledger caption may say "admin services", but if the payment is rent for premises, 194I still needs to be tested.

### Missing the new threshold updates

The Income Tax Department guidance currently reflects a higher threshold under 194J and a monthly test under 194I. Teams using old cheat sheets can easily apply the wrong threshold.

### Ignoring the payee category under 194C

The 1% versus 2% difference under 194C depends on whether the payee is an individual or HUF versus others. Businesses often forget to update vendor masters when entity type changes.

### Using one section for a composite vendor forever

The same vendor can do advisory work in one quarter and contract execution in another. Vendor name does not decide section.

## A practical monthly TDS workflow for founders and accounts teams

If you want fewer notices and fewer vendor complaints, build a repeatable routine.

### Week 1: vendor onboarding

- Collect PAN, legal name, entity type and agreement.
- Tag the vendor as likely 194C, 194I, 194J or "review required".
- Note whether the vendor can issue different invoice types.

### During the month: invoice review

- Match invoice with agreement.
- Check whether the threshold has been crossed in aggregate.
- Escalate mixed invoices before payment release.

### Month-end: deduction and deposit sheet

- Prepare one control sheet showing vendor name, PAN, section, base amount, rate, TDS amount and challan status.
- Have one reviewer sign off unusual entries.

### Quarter-end: return filing

- Reconcile challans and ledger totals.
- File the quarterly statement only after section coding is checked.
- Download Form 16A after processing and share it on time.

This process is not glamorous, but it works. Most businesses do not need a fancy tax engine. They need one clean spreadsheet, one review rule and one accountable owner.

> **Pro tip:** Keep a short "TDS classification matrix" for your top 20 vendor types. New staff can then follow the business rule without reinventing the decision every month.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is software AMC always covered under 194C?

Not always. Some software arrangements are pure licensing or technical support issues, and some are maintenance contracts. Read the agreement first.

### Can office rent ever go under 194C?

Ordinary premises rent is usually first tested under 194I, not 194C. Do not move it merely because the vendor raised a combined facilities invoice.

### What if the vendor does not give PAN?

Higher-rate consequences can arise under the PAN-related TDS rules. Do not postpone onboarding documents until after the first payment.

### If we booked the expense but forgot to deduct TDS, what should we do?

Review the missed entry immediately, calculate the shortfall and interest exposure, and correct it before the quarter closes. Delaying only increases the clean-up work.

### Does one threshold apply vendor-wise or invoice-wise?

It depends on the section. For example, 194C involves both single-payment and aggregate tests, while current departmental guidance for 194I reflects a monthly threshold and 194J reflects an annual threshold for covered categories. Read the section-specific rule carefully.

### Can one monthly rent invoice include furniture and premises in the same bill?

Yes, but the classification should still identify what is being paid for. If separate components are visible, rate analysis becomes more accurate.

## Conclusion

The right way to handle TDS on professional fees, contracts and rent is not to memorise three section numbers and hope for the best. It is to build a classification habit.

Ask what the payment is really for. Check the payer's applicability. Apply the correct threshold and rate. Deduct at the earlier of credit or payment. Deposit on time. File the quarterly statement. Issue Form 16A. Keep the support file.

When businesses do those six things consistently, TDS becomes manageable. When they skip even two of them, the problem usually returns every quarter.

If your team is already seeing confusion between 194C, 194I and 194J, treat that as a process issue, not just a tax issue. Fix the vendor onboarding note, the invoice review step and the month-end control sheet. The savings in time, disputes and correction work are usually far greater than the effort it takes to set that up properly.
